Mutual Futures / Legal reflection

Understand the rules.
Help shape better ones.

Laws affect our work, homes and shared future. This part of the proposal asks how people could understand those rules, see their effects and explore changes through lawful public processes.

Where this approach began

In 2012 and 2013, Luke Nathan Hayes read through Australian legal material to understand its relevance to his own life. He called his method Gather, Chomp, Sort, Plan: collect sources, work through them, organise what matters and decide what to do next. That work informs this proposal for making legal information easier to explore.

Keep these three questions clear

Current obligations and rights

Applicable sources, jurisdiction, commencement, version and unresolved interpretation.

Voluntary commitments

Additional practices an individual or organisation has chosen, clearly distinguished from legal requirements.

Proposed changes

Alternative wording or arrangements, assumptions, consultation and modelled consequences.

Look at how the rules work together

Understanding a legal question may involve national, state and local rules, court decisions, contracts, standards and international agreements. Each source needs its date, version and legal status recorded. The wording of a rule and an interpretation of how it applies should remain clearly identified.

RecordQuestions carried with it
Law and instrumentsWhich jurisdiction, version, commencement and affected provisions?
Judicial interpretationWhich court, issues, reasoning and subsequent treatment?
International commitmentsSignature, ratification, entry into force, reservations and domestic implementation?
Proposed reformWhich rights, duties, institutions and dependencies would change?
Public reflectionWhose questions, alternative models and unresolved disagreements remain visible?

A proposed discussion about Australia's future

Luke proposes a public examination of Australian law and international obligations, leading towards a possible cyber-republic referendum by 2031. In this project, cyber-republic refers to a proposed republic supported by digital tools for public participation and scrutiny. This is his proposed timetable, not an announced referendum.

Any constitutional change would need to follow the actual parliamentary and referendum process. The AEC source explains that process. A model or website cannot make the change itself.

Leave room for different views

People could compare changes with keeping existing arrangements, examine the assumptions and disagree with the proposal. Work, healthcare and member benefits would not depend on sharing a political view. The term radical overcompliance describes the proposal's starting approach: understand and meet current obligations while exploring lawful change.
